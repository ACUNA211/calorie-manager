# Supabase Pro, not AI, sets the bill

The restarted Calorie Manager costs **about $0 a month at its cheapest and about $25 a month in the recommended setup** for one Household of 2–4 Members. Almost all of the $25 is Supabase Pro. **AI costs well under $0.25 a month** in either setup, as long as code does the filtering and arithmetic and the models only pick and rate. Every data source the app needs is free and can be stored permanently: USDA FoodData Central (CC0) for macros and barcodes, Open Food Facts (ODbL) as the fallback for packaged foods, and a Recipe library the Household owns, seeded from public-domain USDA MyPlate Kitchen. Phone barcode scanning is free with a ZXing WebAssembly decoder, push is free with VAPID Web Push, and app-store fees apply only if the Household wants a native iPhone app ($99 a year). The paid alternatives are recipe APIs at $9–$149 a month, barcode SDKs from $99 a month, and the old chat-heavy in-app Agent at about $10 per Member a month. Each one costs more than the whole recommended stack, and the recipe APIs also forbid the permanent storage a Calorie Log needs. Two caveats matter. The research network blocked most vendor sites this round, so only Anthropic's and Google Cloud's AI price pages were read in full; everything else comes from search-engine extracts of official pages, from earlier official checks (2026-09-30 and 2026-10-01), or from third parties. And TypeSafe's Jev is a weeks-old early-access product whose low price is reported, not confirmed on TypeSafe's own pages.

## The restart changes the domain more than the bill

The project vocabulary in CONTEXT.md describes a one-person app. A **Member** is "the one person using the app", household is listed as a term to avoid, the **Pantry** is a "later phase", and the **Agent** is Claude in a chat outside the app, under ADR 0001's decision to use "no AI API key, model choice or AI budget". The restart reverses that ADR in four ways. It adds a **Household** that owns one shared Pantry, Recipe library, Meal Plan and Shopping list. It has 2–4 Members, each with their own login, Calorie Target and Calorie Log. It moves planning into the app on a paid API so a Schedule can draft the week without anyone opening a chat. It turns **Rebalance** from an Agent conversation offering up to three options into a two-step pipeline: Jev rates what the Member said, then fixed formulas in code pick the change. The **Meal Plan** becomes per Household rather than per Member, with each Portion assigned to a Member against that Member's Meal Times. That detail drives the AI costs: one plan covers the whole Household, so planning cost barely grows from 2 Members to 4, while Rebalance cost grows linearly with Members.

The existing Supabase schema is single-member. `member_settings` is a one-row table, and RLS is enabled with zero policies because only the service-role connector touches the data. The restart therefore needs `households` and `household_members` tables, a `household_id` on the shared tables, and membership policies through a `SECURITY DEFINER` helper in a private schema. That avoids the recursion trap of the naive `team_id in (select …)` pattern ([GitHub discussion #4509](https://github.com/orgs/supabase/discussions/4509); [makerkit RLS best practices](https://makerkit.dev/blog/tutorials/supabase-rls-best-practices)). This costs engineering time, not money.

## Food and Recipe data cost $0 and stay storable forever

**USDA FoodData Central is the backbone.** It is free, public domain under **CC0 1.0**, and allows **1,000 requests per hour per IP**. Its Branded Foods type holds about **434,000 US/Canada/NZ packaged products**, with per-serving label macros (`labelNutrients`) and per-100 g values ([FDC API Guide](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/api-guide/); [GBFPD User Guide](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/docs/GBFPD_Documentation_and_Download_User_Guide_Jan2024.pdf)). A Household scanning 200 products a month uses about 0.03% of that limit, and the app's existing `usda_get` Supabase function and Vault key already cover the call. Two technical catches matter more than price. First, FDC has no barcode endpoint, so a scan becomes a full-text search. Stored codes are zero-padded inconsistently, so the app must try the 12-digit and 13-digit forms and accept a hit only when `gtinUpc` matches with leading zeros stripped ([scanner-backend PR #46](https://github.com/JonnyC26/scanner-backend/pull/46); community evidence, not USDA documentation). Second, Foundation Foods dropped energy nutrient 1008 in favour of Atwater IDs 2047/2048, so energy lookups need a fallback chain ([Foundation Foods documentation](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/docs/Foundation_Foods_Documentation_Apr2024.pdf)). Manufacturer errors do occur; the 2026-10-01 live checks found tortillas at both 11 and 619 kcal per 100 g. A 4P+4C+9F sanity check therefore earns its place.

**Open Food Facts is the free fallback.** It shows about **979,000 US products**, many of them imported from FDC, with official limits of **15 product reads and 10 searches per minute per IP** ([OFF API docs](https://openfoodfacts.github.io/openfoodfacts-server/api/); [us.openfoodfacts.org](https://us.openfoodfacts.org/)). Its ODbL share-alike licence is manageable if OFF rows sit unmodified in their own table with on-screen attribution ([OFF terms](https://world.openfoodfacts.org/terms-of-use)). Barcode-only services add product names but not macros. UPCitemdb's free **100 lookups a day** could prefill name and brand after both FDC and OFF miss ([UPCitemdb plans](https://www.upcitemdb.com/wp/docs/main/development/plan/)). Go-UPC starts around $74.95 a month according to a competitor page, which is unverified. The commercial nutrition APIs are ruled out by their terms, not their prices. FatSecret lets you cache only IDs beyond 24 hours ([FatSecret storable data](https://platform.fatsecret.com/docs/guides/storable-data)). Edamam lets paid plans cache only calories, protein, fat and net carbs, behind the user's login ([Edamam Food Database API](https://developer.edamam.com/food-database-api)). Neither fits a permanent shared Pantry.

**Recipe APIs fail the same storage test.** Spoonacular is free at 50 points a day, then $29, $79 or $149 a month. Its terms allow caching for at most one hour, and only with written permission, and only the recipe ID, title and image may be kept indefinitely ([Spoonacular pricing](https://spoonacular.com/food-api/pricing); [Spoonacular terms](https://spoonacular.com/food-api/terms), from an old cached snippet). Edamam Recipe Search costs $9, $99 or $399 a month. It allows macro-only caching, makes "Powered by Edamam" attribution mandatory, requires deleting stored data on cancellation, and gives no cooking instructions on web recipes below the $399 tier ([Edamam Recipe Search API](https://developer.edamam.com/edamam-recipe-api); [Edamam FAQ](https://developer.edamam.com/api/faq)). The large open datasets are research-only: RecipeNLG's 2.2M recipes are "non-commercial research and educational purposes" ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/mbien/recipe_nlg)), and Recipe1M+ is CC BY-NC-SA ([MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/130340)). The cheapest robust path is therefore a Recipe library the Household owns. Ingredients are stored as grams with FDC IDs, matching the existing Recipe definition where calories come from ingredients. Seed the library from **MyPlate Kitchen**, whose USDA/FNS recipes carry full nutrition analysis ([MyPlate About Us](https://MyPlate.gov/about-us); public-domain status of printables at [MyPlate resources](https://MyPlate.gov/resources)), and add Recipes that Members paste in and AI parses. The data cost is $0. The engineering risk moves to ingredient parsing and unit-to-gram matching. MyPlate Kitchen has no API or bulk download, so seeding means a careful, robots.txt-respecting collection.

## A weekly Meal Plan costs about a cent when code does the arithmetic

AI prices have collapsed at the small-model tier. **Claude Haiku 5.5 is $0.10 input / $0.50 output per million tokens**, with $0.01 cache reads and a 50% Batch discount that stacks with caching. Claude 4.7+ tokenizers produce about 30% more tokens for the same text, and US-only inference costs 1.1x ([Claude pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing), read in full). **Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite is $0.25 / $1.50.** **Gemini 3.8 Flash is $0.75 / $3.75 only "through December 31, 2026"** and then doubles to $1.50 / $7.50 ([Google Cloud Gemini pricing](https://cloud.google.com/gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/generative-ai/pricing), read in full). OpenAI's gpt-6-luna ($0.10 / $0.50), gpt-5.4-nano ($0.20 / $1.25) and gpt-oss-20b on Together ($0.05 / $0.20) come from LiteLLM's price file, which cites the provider pages but is secondary ([LiteLLM prices](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/BerriAI/litellm/main/model_prices_and_context_window.json)). DeepSeek V4 Flash has two conflicting price sets and should not be relied on yet.

The cost per plan depends far more on design than on model. The estimates below come from a token model in the notes, not from measurements. In a **lean design**, code pre-filters candidate Recipes by Meal Time calorie range, Pantry fit and dislikes, and the model only assigns about 30–60 Recipe IDs to slots as compact JSON: about 6K tokens in and 2K out. A **typical design** sends about 12K in and 3K out and allows two revisions. The lean design is 4–8x cheaper and easier to validate, because the model can only choose IDs that code has already checked.

| Model (price status) | Lean plan | Typical plan (2 revisions) | Typical, per month (4.34 plans) |
|---|---|---|---|
| gpt-oss-20b, Together (secondary) | $0.0007 | $0.0038 | $0.016 |
| gpt-5-nano (secondary) | $0.0011 | $0.0045 | $0.020 |
| gpt-6-luna (secondary) | $0.0016 | $0.0066 | $0.029 |
| **Claude Haiku 5.5 (primary, x1.3 tokenizer)** | **$0.0021** | **$0.0086** | **$0.037** |
| gpt-5.4-nano (secondary) | $0.0037 | $0.0149 | $0.065 |
| Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite (primary) | $0.0045 | $0.0181 | $0.079 |

| Claude Sonnet 5.5 (primary) | $0.0416 | $0.1695 | $0.736 |

Output and reasoning tokens make up 55–75% of each plan's cost, so capping reasoning effort and returning IDs rather than names are the biggest savings. Batch halves only the scheduled first draft, because revisions are interactive (pricing per [Claude pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing); per-plan figures are the notes' arithmetic). For comparison, the September design's chat-heavy in-app Agent cost about $10 per Member per month on gpt-5.4-mini. About 85% of that came from daily chat logging and Rebalance, not planning (the project's own 2026-09-30 cost model in `.scratch/mvp/research/running-cost.md`, priced from [OpenAI pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing)). Removing the chat Agent is therefore the single largest AI saving in the restart, far larger than any choice of model. Free tiers are not worth taking. Gemini's free tier uses submitted content "to improve our products", which is a poor trade for Household diet data ([Gemini pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing), from the 09-30 check). Groq's reported 8K tokens-per-minute free cap is smaller than a typical 12K plan prompt ([benchlm Groq free tier](https://benchlm.ai/free-tier/groq), third-party). The ADR's Claude-subscription route still costs $0 extra and the skills already exist. But it cannot run from a Schedule, and it ties the whole Household's planning to one Member's personal account, so it fits best as an optional "re-plan with me" path rather than the weekly engine.

## Jev makes Rebalance nearly free but cannot plan

**TypeSafe AI's Jev** launched in early access on 2026-09-15. It is a "System One" decision model: it takes a text state plus typed questions (Choice of up to 255 options, Score on 2–10 ordered levels, Noul yes/no) and returns per-option probabilities and a confidence. It generates no text ([letsdatascience](https://letsdatascience.com/news/typesafe-ai-launches-jev-decision-model-889a38c0); [layer3labs limits](https://www.layer3labs.io/guides/jev-limits)). Its reported price is **$0.042 per million input tokens with output free**, the same on OpenRouter and ZenMux ([eesel.ai](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/typesafe-jev-pricing); [OpenRouter Jev 1.14](https://openrouter.ai/typesafe/jev-1.14)). A Rebalance call carries the Member's message, a compact day log and four questions: meal type, a five-level "how far over" Score, preferred fix, and whether the Member gave a number. That is about 1,500 tokens, or **$0.00006 per call**. At 1–2 Rebalances per Member per day, that comes to **about $0.004–0.008 a month for 2 Members and $0.015 for 4** (my arithmetic on the reported price). Jev is "not a calculator" ([layer3labs limits](https://www.layer3labs.io/guides/jev-limits)), so an "I ate about 1,800 kcal" figure must be parsed in code, and the fixed formulas should use the Score bucket's midpoint or the expected score. This split matches the restart's design: Jev classifies, and code decides the change. The old Rebalance rules carry over as code: never touch Set Meal Times or logged meals, and never take a Planned Meal Time below its range.

The idea of using Jev to keep planning cheap works only as a gate, not as a replacement. Scoring each candidate Recipe in its own call (about 300 calls per plan) costs about **$0.055 a month**, roughly six times the whole lean Haiku plan. A single batched call carrying all candidates as parallel questions costs about **$0.0005 per plan**, but the maximum number of questions per request is unconfirmed. The real saving is skipping a revision when Jev's fit confidence is high. Independent replays show Jev-to-LLM cascades cut cost to **16–48%** of the best single judge while losing **0.2–1.8 points** of accuracy ([arXiv 2609.29769](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.29769v1)). At a cent per plan there is almost nothing left to save, so the gate is optional.

Quality, access and maturity carry more risk than price. Independent tests put Jev "level with mid-price LLMs, behind the frontier" ([DEV, aws-builders](https://dev.to/aws-builders/jev-after-eight-days-of-independent-tests-level-with-mid-price-llms-behind-the-frontier-1c60)). One study found it calibrated when pooled but not within individual benchmarks (median per-benchmark ECE 0.168 against 0.074 under perfect calibration) ([arXiv 2609.29429](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.29429)). A spam test found a cheap LLM better calibrated ([Medium, pravvich](https://medium.com/@pravvich/typesafes-jev-beyond-the-hype-an-independent-benchmark-8bdc1c99d000)). No evaluation covers food or portion text. Reports conflict on access: one says the waitlist ended with a $5 credit, another that new signups paused on September 22 ([explainx.ai](https://www.explainx.ai/blog/jev-general-availability-no-waitlist-2026); [aiprofitboardroom](https://aiprofitboardroom.com/blog/how-to-get-access-to-jev/)). The SDK and raw JSON shapes already differ, and OpenRouter requires a separate Decisions endpoint ([OpenRouter Jev tutorial](https://openrouter.ai/docs/guides/community/jev-tutorial)). The safe build is a small "decision" interface with Jev behind it, a Haiku 5.5 structured-output fallback that costs about $0.05 a month at 4 Members, and a clarify-threshold tuned on about 50 real Household messages.

## Free hosting covers the scale, and Pro buys reliability

**Supabase Free** offers a 500 MB database, 50,000 MAU, 5 GB egress, pg_cron and RLS. That covers a 2–4 Member Household by orders of magnitude, but a Free project **pauses after a week of too little activity and has no backups** ([Supabase pricing](https://supabase.com/pricing); [project pausing](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/platform/free-project-pausing)). **Supabase Pro is $25 a month** and includes 8 GB of database, a $10 compute credit covering one Micro project, daily backups kept 7 days, no pausing, and a spend cap on by default. A `dev` project in the same organisation adds about $10 ([Supabase compute](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/platform/manage-your-usage/compute)). The Supabase figures match the official page as checked on 2026-09-30 and this round's search extracts. Automatic scheduling stays at $0: a per-minute pg_cron job reads a Schedules table (pg_cron runs on UTC) and POSTs due jobs, such as the weekly draft and Meal Time reminders, through `pg_net` ([Supabase Cron](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/cron)). Its run history is never purged automatically, so a nightly cleanup job protects the 500 MB limit.

**Vercel Hobby** costs $0 but is restricted to "non-commercial, personal use", and its cron runs at most once a day. That does not matter here, because pg_cron does the scheduling ([Vercel Hobby](https://vercel.com/docs/plans/hobby); [Vercel cron pricing](https://vercel.com/docs/cron-jobs/usage-and-pricing)). If the app is ever sold to other Households, the cheapest commercial option is Cloudflare Workers Paid at $5 a month ([Workers pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/platform/pricing/)); Vercel Pro costs $20 per seat. Push is free everywhere. VAPID Web Push works on iPhone only after the web app is **added to the Home Screen** on iOS 16.4+ and the permission request follows a tap ([WebKit](https://webkit.org/blog/13878/web-push-for-web-apps-on-ios-and-ipados/)), and FCM is free on every Firebase plan ([Firebase pricing plans](https://firebase.google.com/docs/projects/billing/firebase-pricing-plans)).

Barcode scanning has one trap. The native `BarcodeDetector` is **"disabled by default" on iOS Safari through iOS 26.6** ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/mdn-api_barcodedetector)), so the PWA needs the free `barcode-detector` polyfill built on ZXing-C++ WebAssembly. It uses native detection on Android Chrome and WASM elsewhere, and the `.wasm` should be self-hosted for offline use ([barcode-detector README](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/barcode-detector@3.1.3/README.md)). Paid SDKs cost more than the whole stack: Dynamsoft starts at $1,499 a year ([Dynamsoft pricing](https://www.dynamsoft.com/barcode-reader/pricing/)) and STRICH at a reported $99 a month ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/10020686/STRICH/pricing/)). Native apps are optional. They cost **$99 a year for Apple** (which TestFlight also requires) and **$25 once for Google Play**, both from third-party summaries ([appradar](https://appradar.com/blog/google-play-apple-app-store-fees)). Expo EAS Free gives 15 iOS and 15 Android builds a month ([Expo billing plans](https://docs.expo.dev/billing/plans/)). For 2–4 known Members, the PWA's gaps (Home Screen install for push, WASM scanning) add onboarding steps but block nothing.

## Monthly totals for one Household

The table prices both setups for 2 and 4 Members. "Cheapest" uses Supabase Free, lean planning on the cheapest model with an adequate schema, and Jev for Rebalance. "Recommended" uses Supabase Pro, Haiku 5.5 for typical planning with revisions (the only small model priced on a primary page this round, with strong schema support), Jev for Rebalance with a Haiku fallback, an optional batched Jev gate, and Haiku parsing about 30 pasted Recipes a month into grams. That last figure is my estimate: about 2.6K tokens in and 1.3K out per Recipe, about $0.03 a month.

Three add-ons change the total. A native iPhone app adds about **$8.25 a month** ($99 a year), plus $25 once for Android. A `dev` project inside the Pro organisation adds about **$10**; running `dev` locally with the Supabase CLI avoids it. Commercial hosting adds $5 or $20. A **$2 a month hard cap** on the AI provider key, with an in-app usage ledger, covers about 10x the expected heavy-month AI spend, and Anthropic supports a self-set spend limit ([Claude rate limits](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/rate-limits), checked 2026-09-30). The cheapest setup's only real risks are pausing and the lack of backups. Daily Household use plus a per-minute pg_cron job that writes rows very likely prevents pausing, but that rests on a non-staff forum answer ([GitHub discussion #13121](https://github.com/orgs/supabase/discussions/13121)).

## Conclusion

The restart's spending risk lies in what the AI is asked to do, not in which vendor is chosen. Once the in-app chat Agent is replaced by a scheduled, schema-constrained planner and a Jev classifier feeding fixed formulas, AI drops from the largest line (about $10 per Member in September's design) to rounding error. That leaves a $25 database subscription, and its job is backups and no pausing, not capacity. So the real choice is whether a Household's shared Calorie Log is worth $300 a year of insurance. Every AI choice costs less than a dollar a year, so models can be picked on quality and schema reliability alone.

The same logic reframes Jev. Its value is not its price, which hardly matters at this scale. Its value is that Rebalance becomes auditable: probabilities and a confidence go into a formula the Household can read, instead of an LLM's free-form suggestions. That is worth building behind a swappable interface, because Jev's access status, schema and calibration on food text are all unproven, and a cheap LLM fallback costs almost nothing to keep ready.
